# 13-month-calendar (暦　koyomi)
 Middle English kalender, from Old French calendier, from Latin calendarium (“account book”)
 
## 13month calendar actively try to use by US before and during WW2 ?
Yes, a 13-month calendar was actively used internally by major US businesses, and there was a massive push to adopt it nationwide just before World War II.
Known as the International Fixed Calendar (or the Cotsworth Plan), this system divided the year into 13 identical months of exactly 28 days each, totaling 364 days. The extra month, called "Sol," was placed between June and July. To reach 365 days, a blank "Year Day" holiday was added at the very end of the year outside of any week.
The calendar's use and the movement to adopt it during this era evolved through key stages:

### 1. Active Corporate Use (Eastman Kodak)

The most prominent corporate adopter was the Eastman Kodak Company, which officially implemented the 13-month calendar for its internal operations, accounting, payroll, and production schedules in 1928. Kodak founder George Eastman was a passionate believer that the Gregorian calendar's uneven months distorted financial bookkeeping and business trends. Kodak continued using this system through the Great Depression, all of World War II, and up until 1989. Over 100 other American companies and prominent businessmen (including hotelier E.M. Statler) also adopted it or strongly endorsed it during the interwar period.

### 2. The US Political Push Before WWII

Throughout the 1920s and early 1930s, the movement nearly became US law:
• Congressional Proposals: Multiple joint resolutions were introduced in the US House of Representatives calling for an international conference to officially adopt the 13-month calendar.
• The International Fixed Calendar League: Backed by George Eastman's funding, the league opened its US headquarters right out of Rochester, New York, to lobby the government and global bodies.
